This is a compiled research record — the product of multiple deep-dive research passes across academic journals, government reports, court records, encyclopedias, organizational publications, news archives, and psychological studies. Everything here is sourced. Nothing is invented.

The goal is to understand, in full detail, what the words "antisemite" and "antisemitic" actually mean, where they came from, how they have been used, who uses them, why they use them, and what happens when they do. This includes the term's genuine utility in identifying hatred against Jews, as well as its documented misuse as a political weapon.

We do not adopt the theories of others. We gather clues and evidence. We follow the money, the names, the dates, the documents, and the patterns. Where there are gaps in the record, we say so. Where there are anomalies — things that do not fit the standard narrative — we flag them.

## Part I: Where the Word Came From

### 1.1 Before There Was "Antisemitism"

For most of history, there was no word "antisemitism." Hatred of Jews existed, often violently, but it was described in religious terms. The German word *Judenhass* meant simply "Jew-hatred." It was crude, theological, and associated with medieval Christian hostility: the belief that Jews had killed Jesus, the blood libel (the false accusation that Jews murdered Christian children to use their blood in rituals), forced conversions, expulsions, and pogroms.

*Judenhass* was real, it was deadly, and it was widespread. But by the mid-19th century, something was changing in Europe. The old religious framework was losing ground to something new: "scientific racism." European intellectuals began classifying human beings into "races" based on physical and linguistic characteristics. The term "Aryan" was invented to describe a supposed master race of northern Europeans. And philologists — scholars who study languages — created the category "Semitic" to group together Hebrew, Arabic, Aramaic, and Amharic.

Here is the first anomaly: there was never a "Semitic people." There were only Semitic languages. No parent Semitic language was ever found. The category was a linguistic convenience that became, in the hands of racial theorists, a biological fiction. This matters because the word "antisemitism" is built on this fiction.

### 1.2 Wilhelm Marr: The Man Who Coined the Term

The word "antisemitism" was invented by one man: Wilhelm Marr, a German nationalist, journalist, and political agitator, in March 1879.

Marr was born in 1819 in Magdeburg, Germany. His father was a well-known theater personality. Marr began his political life as a democratic revolutionary, involved in the failed 1848 revolutions across Europe. He favored the emancipation of oppressed groups, including Jews. He was expelled from Switzerland for his left-wing activities and spent a decade in exile in North and Central America. When he returned to Germany, something had changed in him. He was embittered by the failure of the revolutions, by his own declining political fortunes, and he turned his venom specifically against Jews.

In March 1879, Marr published a 50-page pamphlet with a dramatic title: *Der Sieg des Judenthums über das Germanenthum* — "The Victory of Judaism over Germandom, Considered from a Non-Confessional Standpoint." The subtitle was *Vae Victis!* — "Woe to the vanquished!" The pamphlet went through twelve editions in a single year. It was a publishing phenomenon.

What made Marr's pamphlet different from centuries of *Judenhass* was not the hatred itself — that was old. What was new was the packaging. Marr explicitly rejected the old Christian religious framework. His pamphlet was "non-confessional." He wanted Jew-hatred to sound modern, scientific, and intellectual. He borrowed the term "Semitic" from philology and turned it into a racial category. Jews, he argued, were not just a religious group to be converted or despised for theological reasons. They were a "Semitic race" locked in mortal biological conflict with the German "Aryan race."

In 1880, Marr published a follow-up: *Der Weg zum Siege des Germanenthums über das Judenthum* — "The Road to Victory of Germanness over Jewishness." On September 26, 1879, he founded the *Antisemiten-Liga* — the League of Antisemites — in Berlin. This was the first political organization whose sole purpose was organized hatred of Jews, and it was here that the term "Antisemitismus" was first used as a political program.

The term spread rapidly. Within a year, it was being used across Europe. It gave Jew-hatred a veneer of respectability. It sounded like a scientific position, not a medieval prejudice.

Here is the second anomaly: Marr's first three wives were of Jewish lineage. In 1891 — twelve years after he had done more than almost anyone to popularize hatred of Jews — he published a document he called his *Testament of an Antisemite*, in which he apologized for his "mistaken notions." The father of antisemitism recanted. This fact is rarely emphasized in standard accounts, but it is crucial: the man who gave the ideology its name eventually rejected it.

### 1.3 Why the Term Is a Misnomer

The word "antisemitism" implies opposition to Semites — which, if taken literally, would include Arabs, Ethiopians, and others who speak Semitic languages. But the term was never intended to mean that. Marr coined it specifically and solely to mean hatred of Jews. He used "Semitic" as a racial code for "Jewish."

Encyclopedia Britannica — one of the most authoritative reference works in the English language — explicitly calls "antisemitism" a "misnomer" because of this linguistic mismatch. Yet the term has been universally adopted, and attempts to replace it have failed.

The hyphenation debate illustrates the problem. When written as "anti-Semitism," the hyphen suggests two separate concepts: "anti" and "Semitism" — as if "Semitism" were a thing that could be opposed. The International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance (IHRA) now favors the unhyphenated spelling "antisemitism" (lowercase 's') to emphasize that it is a single, unified term, not a compound of two independent ideas. In German, French, Spanish, and many other languages, the term was never hyphenated to begin with.

This matters because the misnomer creates confusion that is actively exploited. A common argument — heard from figures as varied as Mahmoud Abbas and Kanye West — is that Arabs cannot be antisemitic because they are "Semites too." This is an etymological fallacy: using the archaic root of a word to argue about its current meaning. The term was coined in 1879 with one meaning only — hatred of Jews — and that is how it has been used ever since. Arabs can be, and sometimes are, anti-Jewish, and some Arab political discourse has borrowed directly from European antisemitic conspiracy theories (notably Holocaust denial). The linguistic irony does not change the reality.

As the academic Joseph Massad has noted: "I do not believe that anyone is a 'Semite' any more than I believe anyone is an 'Aryan.' Arabs or Jews should not proudly declare that they are 'Semites' because European racists classified them as such."

## Part II: What the Word Means — Competing Definitions

If the term's origin is murky, its definition is even more contested. There is no single, universally accepted definition of antisemitism. Instead, there are competing frameworks, each with its own political implications, and the battle over which definition institutions adopt has become, in itself, a proxy war over Israel and Palestine.

### 2.1 The IHRA Working Definition (2016)

The most widely adopted definition is the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance (IHRA) Working Definition of Antisemitism, adopted on May 26, 2016, in Bucharest.

The core definition is only 38 words:

> "Antisemitism is a certain perception of Jews, which may be expressed as hatred toward Jews. Rhetorical and physical manifestations of antisemitism are directed toward Jewish or non-Jewish individuals and/or their property, toward Jewish community institutions and religious facilities."

This core text is vague by design. It was the only part that IHRA member countries could agree on. The controversy — and the political weight — lies in the eleven "illustrative examples" that accompany it.

**The eleven examples break down as follows:**

Four are general:
1. Calling for, aiding, or justifying the killing or harming of Jews in the name of a radical ideology or an extremist view of religion.
2. Making mendacious, dehumanizing, demonizing, or stereotypical allegations about Jews as such or the power of Jews as collective — such as, especially but not exclusively, the myth about a world Jewish conspiracy or of Jews controlling the media, economy, government or other societal institutions.
3. Accusing Jews as a people of being responsible for real or imagined wrongdoing committed by a single Jewish person or group, or even for acts committed by non-Jews.
4. Denying the fact, scope, mechanisms (e.g. gas chambers) or intentionality of the genocide of the Jewish people at the hands of National Socialist Germany and its supporters and accomplices during World War II (the Holocaust).

Seven relate to Israel:
5. Accusing the Jews as a people, or Israel as a state, of inventing or exaggerating the Holocaust.
6. Accusing Jewish citizens of being more loyal to Israel, or to the alleged priorities of Jews worldwide, than to the interests of their own nations.
7. Denying the Jewish people their right to self-determination, e.g., by claiming that the existence of a State of Israel is a racist endeavor.
8. Applying double standards by requiring of it a behavior not expected or demanded of any other democratic nation.
9. Using the symbols and images associated with classic antisemitism (e.g., claims of Jews killing Jesus or blood libel) to characterize Israel or Israelis.
10. Drawing comparisons of contemporary Israeli policy to that of the Nazis.
11. Holding Jews collectively responsible for actions of the state of Israel.

The definition includes a critical caveat: "Criticism of Israel similar to that leveled against any other country cannot be regarded as antisemitic."

**Status and adoption:** The IHRA definition is explicitly non-legally binding. Yet it has been adopted by over 35 countries and more than 1,100 entities — universities, municipalities, sports organizations, and corporations. Its adoption by institutions creates what critics call a "de facto speech code" — not a law, but a policy framework that shapes what can and cannot be said without professional or social consequence.

**The examples controversy:** The IHRA member countries reached consensus on the definition only by excluding the examples from the definition itself. In the original document, the definition and examples were printed in distinct typefaces; the examples were physically demarcated as separate. Germany, which adopted an "extended version" in September 2017, incorporated none of the examples — yet IHRA publicly recognized this as a valid adoption.

Pro-Israel activists, including the Simon Wiesenthal Center, consider the core 38-word definition "neutered, unmoored from any current reality." The American Jewish Committee (AJC) has gone so far as to designate the examples as "essentially the definition." Seven of the eleven examples relate to Israel. Critics say this reveals the real purpose: not to define hatred of Jews, but to police criticism of Israel.

A 2021 study published on PubMed concluded that the IHRA definition and its examples "ought to be rejected" due to intrinsic problems: ambiguous wording about "the power of Jews as a collective," lack of clarity on what "right to self-determination" means in this context, and the framework's structure tending to deny the existence of obvious racism against non-Jewish groups. The study preferred the Oxford English Dictionary definition: "hostility to or prejudice against Jews" — with the critical addition of "as Jews."

### 2.2 The Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism (JDA) — The Alternative

In March 2021, a group of scholars at the Van Leer Jerusalem Institute published the Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism (JDA) as an alternative — or, in their framing, a supplement — to IHRA.

The JDA's core definition is starkly simpler:

> "Antisemitism is discrimination, prejudice, hostility or violence against Jews as Jews (or Jewish institutions as Jewish)."

This is sixteen words. It contains no mention of Israel. The JDA then provides fifteen guidelines divided into three sections:

**Section A — General manifestations (5 guidelines):** Holocaust denial, Rothschild conspiracy theories, blood libel, and general racist tropes about Jews.

**Section B — Israel/Palestine, inherently antisemitic (5 guidelines):** Holding Jews collectively responsible for Israel's actions; requiring Jews to publicly disavow Israel or Zionism; denying the right of Jews in the State of Israel to exist and flourish as Jews; treating Jews as "white" when targeted by white supremacists; using classic antisemitic tropes to characterize Israel or Israelis.

**Section C — Israel/Palestine, NOT necessarily antisemitic (5 guidelines):** Expressing support for Palestinians; comparing Israel with settler-colonialism and apartheid; opposition to Zionism; boycotts, divestments, and sanctions aimed at Israel; proposing different political arrangements such as a one-state solution.

The JDA's FAQ clarifies that signatories have different views on BDS (Boycott, Divestment, Sanctions). Guideline 14 states only that BDS is not, in and of itself, antisemitic. The document does not take an explicit stance for or against a one-state or two-state solution.

**Reception:** Some progressive Democrats in the US House of Representatives urged the State Department to adopt the JDA alongside IHRA. The State Department rejected this and maintains its embrace of IHRA "in its entirety, including its examples." The JDA has been criticized by pro-Israel groups like the BESA Center for undermining consensus, and praised by Palestinian solidarity advocates for protecting legitimate political speech.

### 2.3 The Nexus Document

Created in 2019-2020 by the Nexus Task Force at Bard College's Center for the Study of Hate, the Nexus Document is positioned as a complement to IHRA — its creators described it as the "Gemara to IHRA's Mishnah" (referencing the layered structure of Jewish religious texts).

Its definition reads: "Antisemitism consists of anti-Jewish beliefs, attitudes, actions, or systemic conditions... As an embodiment of collective Jewish organization and action, Israel is a magnet for and a target of antisemitic behavior."

The Nexus Document attempts to thread a needle: acknowledging that criticism of Israel can be antisemitic while protecting most forms of Palestinian advocacy.

### 2.4 The Academic Critique

Beyond these three institutional frameworks, academic philosophers and legal scholars have raised fundamental questions about whether any definition of antisemitism can be both precise and politically neutral.

The 2021 PubMed study identified what it called "intrinsic problems" with the IHRA framework: the ambiguous phrase "power of Jews as a collective" could be read as suggesting that any discussion of Jewish political influence is inherently antisemitic; the reference to "right to self-determination" is legally and philosophically contested; and the framework's structure tends to deny the existence of obvious racism against non-Jewish groups by framing antisemitism as a unique category.

The Oxford English Dictionary's simple definition — "hostility to or prejudice against Jews" — remains the most widely cited academic reference, though it lacks the institutional weight of IHRA.

## Part III: The Historical Evolution — From Judenhass to the Present

### 3.1 The Religious Era: Christian Anti-Judaism

Before Marr, hatred of Jews was primarily theological. Medieval Christianity taught that Jews were guilty of deicide — the killing of God. This was not racial; it was religious. A Jew who converted to Christianity was, in theory, no longer a Jew in the eyes of the Church. The hostility took the form of forced conversions, expulsions, the blood libel, and pogroms.

Some scholars, such as Robert Morgan, argue for maintaining a sharp distinction between this "Christian anti-Judaism" (opposition to Judaism as a religion) and modern "antisemitism" (hostility to Jews as a racial or ethnic group). Others, such as Susannah Heschel, argue that this distinction "masks rather than illuminates" — that racial language was used against Jews in the Middle Ages, and that Nazi-era theologians integrated race and religion with disturbing fluidity.

Pope John Paul II maintained the distinction, stating that theological anti-Judaism is different from modern antisemitism and that Christian teachings should not be held responsible for the Holocaust. The historical reality is more complex: Christian anti-Judaism provided the foundation, the vocabulary, and the cultural habit of Jew-hatred upon which modern racial antisemitism was built.

### 3.2 The Racial Era: Marr to the Nazis

Marr's innovation was to strip Jew-hatred of its religious clothing and dress it in the language of 19th-century "scientific racism." This was the era of Social Darwinism, eugenics, and racial hierarchies. The "Aryan race" was imagined as noble, creative, and destined to rule. The "Semitic race" was imagined as parasitic, destructive, and alien.

This framework reached its horrific culmination in Nazi Germany. The Nazis did not invent antisemitism; they industrialized it. The Holocaust was the logical endpoint of a racial ideology that classified Jews as subhuman. The term "antisemitism" itself was used by the Nazis — it was, after all, their word.

### 3.3 The Soviet Era: Anti-Zionism as Antisemitism

After the 1967 Six-Day War, the Soviet Union intensified a campaign that equated Zionism with racism and Nazism. This was a Cold War tactic: by attacking Israel, the Soviets could deflect US criticism of Soviet antisemitism.

The culmination was UN General Assembly Resolution 3379, passed in 1975, which declared that "Zionism is a form of racism and racial discrimination." This was a Soviet-Arab bloc product. It was revoked in 1991 after the Cold War ended, with UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan later calling it a "low point" in the organization's history.

The method was insidious: Soviet anti-Zionist propaganda "drew heavily on far-right, racist ideas" but packaged them in a left-wing ideological framework. The same conspiracy theories about Jewish world domination that had circulated in Nazi Germany were repackaged as "anti-imperialist" critique. This created a lasting confusion: legitimate criticism of Israeli policy became entangled with recycled antisemitic tropes.

The 2001 World Conference Against Racism in Durban, South Africa, marked another inflection point. NGOs at the parallel forum branded Israel a "racist, apartheid state," and the Boycott, Divestment, Sanctions (BDS) movement was institutionalized with this framing.

### 3.4 The "New Antisemitism" — A Propaganda Concept?

In 1974, ADL leaders Arnold Forster and Benjamin Epstein published a book titled *The New Anti-Semitism*. This was one of the first systematic attempts to identify anti-Zionism as a new form of antisemitism.

The book's contents are revealing. Of its 324 pages, only 24 focused on antisemitism from the political right. It absolved Christianity of any role in antisemitism. It framed Jews as representatives of liberalism and democracy targeted by a "new totalitarianism" of Black Power and Third World revolution. It devoted a chapter to the 1968 New York teachers' strike, attributing antisemitic rhetoric (on scant evidence) to the African Teachers Association.

The concept was not entirely new. Precedents include Jean Améry's 1969 essay "Virtuous Antisemitism," ADL chair David A. Rose's 1971 warning that anti-Israel campaigns were "in their broadest sense, anti-Jewish," and Israeli Foreign Minister Abba Eban's 1973 statement that "anti-Zionism is merely the new anti-Semitism."

Critics — including Norman Finkelstein and others — have argued that the "new antisemitism" concept was not an organic discovery by Jewish communities but a coordinated campaign by Zionist groups and the Israeli government to stamp out attacks on Israel from the left. In this view, it functioned as a form of "counterinsurgency" against Palestinian solidarity movements.

Academic critics note that the concept was accompanied by what Mark Cohen called a "neo-lachrymose conception of Jewish-Arab history" — a revisionist narrative emphasizing mistreatment of Jews in Islamic lands, developed specifically to rebut Arab claims that Zionism had ruined an idyllic Jewish-Muslim coexistence.

### 3.5 The BDS Movement and the Term

The Boycott, Divestment, Sanctions (BDS) movement, formally launched in 2005 by Palestinian civil society organizations, calls for economic and cultural pressure on Israel until it meets three demands: ending the occupation of Palestinian territories, recognizing the rights of Palestinian citizens of Israel to full equality, and respecting the right of return for Palestinian refugees.

The movement has become one of the primary battlegrounds over the term "antisemitism." Pro-Israel groups and governments, including the ADL and the US State Department, have labeled BDS as inherently antisemitic, arguing that it singles out the world's only Jewish state for condemnation while ignoring worse human rights abuses elsewhere. They point to the movement's founders' refusal to acknowledge Israel's right to exist and to instances of BDS activists targeting Jewish cultural events, Israeli academics regardless of their political views, and Jewish-owned businesses.

BDS supporters, including many Jewish organizations, argue that the movement is a legitimate nonviolent protest tactic against a military occupation, modeled on the anti-apartheid movement in South Africa. They note that boycotts are a time-honored form of political speech protected by international law and that conflating criticism of Israeli policy with hatred of Jews is itself a tactic to silence dissent. The JDA explicitly states that BDS is not, in and of itself, antisemitic.

The IHRA definition does not mention BDS specifically, but its Example 8 — "Applying double standards by requiring of it a behavior not expected or demanded of any other democratic nation" — is frequently invoked against BDS activists. The European Legal Support Center found that accusations of antisemitism against Palestinian solidarity groups often cited this example.

The BDS debate illustrates the central tension in modern usage of the term: when does political opposition to a state become hostility to a people? The answer depends almost entirely on which definition one adopts.

### 3.6 The Social Media Era

The internet and social media have transformed how antisemitism spreads. A 2020-2021 study found a 41% increase in antisemitic posts on TikTok, a 912% increase in antisemitic comments, and a 1,375% increase in antisemitic usernames. On Instagram, millions of results appeared for antisemitic conspiracy hashtags. Facebook removed only 10.9% of reported antisemitic posts in one study. Telegram has become the primary refuge for extremists.

The tactics have evolved: emojis used as code (watermelons, paragliders, Palestinian flags), memes repurposing popular formats, AI-generated antisemitic images, Holocaust denial, and the revival of blood libel accusations. Social media algorithms prioritize engagement, and outrageous content gets amplified, creating dopamine feedback loops that incentivize hate.

After October 7, 2023, researchers documented what they called an "abandonment of justification" — hatred was no longer masked as political critique; it was asserted as spectacle.

## Part IV: Key Historical Cases and Tropes

### 4.1 The Dreyfus Affair (1894-1906)

The Dreyfus Affair was the trial of Alfred Dreyfus, a French Jewish army officer falsely accused of treason. It became one of the defining events of modern antisemitism and a catalyst for political Zionism.

France was considered by some historians to be the "cradle of antisemitic ideology." The affair polarized French society, with Émile Zola's famous "J'Accuse" letter becoming one of the first examples of an intellectual publicly intervening in a political injustice. Theodor Herzl, then a journalist covering the trial, later wrote that the affair convinced him of the need for a Jewish state — though scholars debate whether this was the sole catalyst or merely a confirmation of ideas he had already been developing.

The affair also marked the transition from religious anti-Judaism to modern political antisemitism in public discourse. The "Jewish Question" — whether Jews could ever truly be citizens of European nations — became a central political issue.

### 4.2 The Leo Frank Case and the Founding of the ADL (1913)

In 1913, Leo Frank, a Jewish factory manager in Atlanta, Georgia, was convicted of the murder of Mary Phagan, a thirteen-year-old Christian girl. The trial was the longest criminal trial in Southern history at that time. The jury voted unanimously to convict. The grand jury, which included four Jews, had voted 21-0 to indict.

Frank fought a two-year appeals process through every level of the Georgia and federal court systems. All rejected his claims of an unfair trial. In 1915, Governor John Slaton commuted Frank's death sentence to life imprisonment. On August 17, 1915, a mob kidnapped Frank from prison and lynched him in Marietta, Georgia. He remains the only Jew ever lynched in America, and the first white man in the Deep South brought to trial on a capital charge based solely on the testimony of a Black man (Jim Conley, the factory's janitor).

Frank received a posthumous pardon in 1986 — not an exoneration — based on 1982 testimony from Alonzo Mann, who had been an office boy at the factory.

The Anti-Defamation League (ADL) was founded in October 1913 by Sigmund Livingston, under the sponsorship of B'nai B'rith. The ADL officially states that the Leo Frank case was not the impetus — Livingston had conceived of the organization earlier. But the case "dramatically reinforced the belief in the wisdom of creating an ADL." Hundreds of Jewish sources since 1913 have stated that Frank's trial and conviction led directly to the ADL's founding. Frank was, at the time of his arrest, president of the Atlanta chapter of B'nai B'rith, and the chapter re-elected him unanimously while he was incarcerated.

Here is an anomaly: Mary Phagan's great-niece, Mary Phagan-Kean, maintains that Frank was guilty and has criticized the ADL's century-long campaign to exonerate him as ideologically motivated. She claims the ADL has worked for over a century to "rewrite history." This creates a strange situation where the foundational case of one of the world's most prominent anti-hate organizations is contested by the victim's own family.

Here is another anomaly: recent scholarship by Emmaia Gelman questions whether the ADL was founded in response to the Leo Frank case at all, suggesting instead that it was formed by midwestern German Jews worried about Eastern European Jewish immigrants changing perceptions of Jewishness. The origin myth may be just that — a myth.

### 4.3 The Protocols of the Elders of Zion

The *Protocols of the Elders of Zion* is perhaps the most influential antisemitic forgery in history. Created in the late 1890s by the Russian Tsarist secret police (the Okhrana), likely by agent Matvei Golovinski, it was first published in 1903 in the Russian newspaper *Znamya*, edited by the antisemitic agitator Pavel Krushevan.

The document purported to be the minutes of secret meetings of Jewish leaders at the First Zionist Congress in Basel, Switzerland, in 1897, detailing a plot for world domination through control of finance, media, government, education, and revolutionary movements. It contained 24 chapters or "protocols."

The forgery was exposed multiple times:
- In 1921, Philip Graves of *The Times* (London) published side-by-side comparisons showing that the Protocols were plagiarized from Maurice Joly's 1864 French satire *Dialogue in Hell Between Machiavelli and Montesquieu* — a critique of Napoleon III that contained no Jewish content. Over 160 passages were directly lifted.
- In 1935, a Swiss court in the Berne Trial ruled the Protocols a forgery and plagiarism.
- In 1937, a Basel court confirmed the ruling.
- In 1938, Russian journalist Vladimir Burtsev published a book exposing the forgery.
- US Senate reports in 1964 confirmed the plagiarism.

Despite this, the Protocols became central to Nazi propaganda. Hitler cited them in *Mein Kampf*. Joseph Goebbels called them a forgery but believed in their "inner truth." Nazi Germany assigned distillations of the Protocols to schoolchildren. The Nazi newspaper *Der Stürmer* used Protocols imagery extensively. The document continues to circulate in extremist circles, in parts of the Arab world, and online to this day.

### 4.4 The Blood Libel

The blood libel — the false accusation that Jews murder Christian children to use their blood in religious rituals, particularly in the baking of matzah for Passover — is often called the "ur-conspiracy theory" from which all other antisemitic conspiracy theories descend.

The first major case emerged in 1144 in Norwich, England, where the death of a boy named William was blamed on Jews as part of a local church's effort to build a cult of martyrdom. In the 13th century, the charge spread to the European continent, and the "blood motif" was added: Jews were accused of killing Christian children specifically to use their blood.

Significant cases include the Simon of Trent affair in Italy (1475), the Damascus Affair in Ottoman Syria (1840), the Kishinev Pogrom in the Russian Empire (1903), and the Beilis Affair in the Russian Empire (1913).

The blood libel was repurposed by Nazi Germany and has experienced a revival in Middle Eastern propaganda and on social media. Modern coded versions include claims that a "secretive elite" harvests "adrenochrome" from children, or that Jewish Israelis "intentionally harvest organs of Palestinians." Jewish Studies scholar Magda Teter has documented how print media transformed these rumors into near-facts in the public mind. The Kielce pogrom in post-WWII Poland (1946) was triggered by the outrageous assertion that Jews had "used the blood of Christian children" as an ingredient in ritual bread.

### 4.5 The Dual Loyalty Trope

The accusation that Jews are more loyal to Israel or to a global Jewish conspiracy than to their own nations is one of the oldest and most persistent antisemitic tropes. Its roots lie in medieval Christian Europe, where Jews were suspected of being in league with Muslim powers.

The trope was visible in the Dreyfus Affair and underlay the Nazi claim that Jewish emancipation in Europe had been a failure. In the 1920s, Henry Ford's *The International Jew* alleged that Jews were pushing America toward war for financial gain and world domination.

In contemporary form, the charge manifests as the claim that Jewish citizens are "more loyal to Israel, or to the alleged priorities of Jews worldwide, than to the interests of their own nations." During the Bush administration, spurious accusations — largely from the left — suggested that Jewish "neoconservatives" (Paul Wolfowitz, Scooter Libby, Elliott Abrams, Douglas Feith, Richard Perle) had hijacked US foreign policy for Israel's benefit. None of these individuals were top-tier administration members, and the charge mirrored classic antisemitic conspiracy theories.

The dual loyalty trope is explicitly listed as an example of antisemitism in the IHRA definition (Example 6).

### 4.6 Judeo-Bolshevism

Judeo-Bolshevism was the conspiracy theory that Jews were behind communism and the Bolshevik Revolution. It became a crucial ingredient in Nazi ideology and a direct precursor to the Holocaust.

The trope emerged during the Russian Civil War, when Ukrainian White propaganda portrayed communist enemies as insidious Jewish agents. A 1917 pamphlet declared: "The sun shone in Kiev and the tsar often came here. Now the Jews are everywhere! We want to throw off this yoke... They will destroy the Fatherland. Down with the Jews! Give us back the tsar!"

By the early 1920s, Catholic bishops in Poland were writing open letters describing Bolsheviks as the "living embodiment of the Antichrist on earth." Reprints of the Protocols of the Elders of Zion circulated through Catholic presses in France and Italy, Henry Ford's *Dearborn Independent*, and the British *Morning Post*.

The human cost was staggering. In Ukraine between 1918 and 1921, more than 2,000 pogroms occurred. In Hungary, the 1919 revolution led by Béla Kun (who was Jewish) was followed by a counter-revolutionary fascist purge that murdered approximately 3,000 people — half of them Jews — jailed 70,000 in "concentration camps," and forced 100,000 into exile. In Romania, forces slaughtered 25,000 Jews in Odessa in October 1941 in reprisals against the "Judeo-Bolshevik enemy."

After World War II, the trope persisted in countries under Soviet rule. The Kielce pogrom of 1946 was partly motivated by the belief that "a Jew is a Bolshevik." In West Germany, Chancellor Konrad Adenauer declared that "Asia stands on the Elbe" — conjuring the same fear of "Asiatic Bolshevism" that Goebbels and Hitler had ingrained.

The intellectual tradition persists today in attacks on George Soros, which draw on the same framework of an exploitative global Jewish conspiracy against "pure, humble, and selfless nationalists."

### 4.7 Holocaust Denial and the Term

Holocaust denial — the claim that the Nazi genocide of six million Jews did not occur, or was greatly exaggerated — occupies a unique position in antisemitism discourse because it is simultaneously a form of antisemitism and a meta-commentary on the term itself.

Deniers often argue that the Holocaust is a hoax invented by Jews to extract money, political power, and sympathy — a conspiracy theory that inverts the historical record and accuses the victims of being the perpetrators. This is explicitly listed as an example of antisemitism in the IHRA definition (Example 4).

But Holocaust denial also functions as a test of the term's boundaries. Some deniers claim they are not antisemitic but merely "historical revisionists" pursuing academic truth. This framing has been rejected by every legitimate historical institution and court that has examined it. The evidence for the Holocaust is among the most thoroughly documented in human history.

The connection between Holocaust denial and the term "antisemitism" is that denial is often the entry point for broader antisemitic conspiracy beliefs. Research has shown that belief in Holocaust denial is strongly correlated with belief in other conspiracy theories, including those about Jewish control of media, finance, and government. The denial is not really about history; it is about removing the moral barrier that prevents open hatred of Jews.

In some Arab and Muslim countries, Holocaust denial has been promoted by governments and media as a way to undermine Israel's legitimacy. Mahmoud Abbas, before becoming president of the Palestinian Authority, wrote a doctoral dissertation in 1982 that questioned the number of Jewish victims and suggested Zionist collaboration with the Nazis. He later distanced himself from these claims, but the document remains part of the record.

## Part V: Who Uses the Term and Why

### 5.1 The ADL: Civil Rights Organization or Israel Lobby?

The Anti-Defamation League (ADL) is the most prominent organization associated with the term "antisemitism" in the United States. Understanding its history, funding, and evolution is essential to understanding how the term is deployed in American public life.

**Founding and early history:** Founded in late September or October 1913 by Sigmund Livingston under the sponsorship of the Independent Order of B'nai B'rith, the ADL began with $200 and two desks in Livingston's Chicago law office. Its original mission was "to stop, by appeals to reason and conscience, and if necessary, by appeals to law, the defamation of the Jewish people... to secure justice and fair treatment to all citizens alike... [and] put an end forever to unjust and unfair discrimination against and ridicule of any sect or body of citizens."

In its early decades, the ADL campaigned against Henry Ford's antisemitic propaganda, opposed pro-Nazi activity in the US during the 1930s, worked with the NAACP to support major civil rights legislation, and even opposed McCarthyism during the Cold War.

**The 1970s pivot:** Under National Director Nathan Perlmutter (1973-1987), the ADL underwent a significant shift. It opened a Jerusalem headquarters in 1977 and began systematically identifying anti-Zionism with antisemitism. This was the era of the "new antisemitism" concept, and the ADL was its primary institutional promoter.

**The 1993 spying scandal:** In 1993, San Francisco police raided ADL offices and discovered intelligence files on over 600 civic organizations and more than 10,000 individuals. Approximately 75% of the material had been illegally obtained through secret payments to police officials. An operative named Roy Bullock had worked for the ADL for over 32 years, compiling files on approximately 1,000 groups and 10,000 individuals.

The targets included: Arab Americans, critics of Israel, the NAACP, the ACLU, ACT UP, Greenpeace, the New Jewish Agenda, anti-apartheid activists, and labor organizations. The ADL passed information to the Israeli government, South African apartheid intelligence services, and the FBI. They even tried to find "sexual impropriety" on Desmond Tutu.

The ADL paid $75,000 to settle the criminal case, $175,000 in legal fees, and was permanently enjoined from illegal spying by Federal Judge Richard Paez in 2000. Abraham Foxman, then head of the ADL, contended that the organization had a right to use police information to combat antisemitism and called allegations that the ADL was acting for Israel "antisemitic."

**Current operations:** Today, the ADL has an annual budget of approximately $134 million (2024). It spent $1.6 million in the first quarter of 2024 alone on IHRA-related lobbying. Combined with the AJC and Robert Kraft's Foundation to Combat Antisemitism, the three organizations represent roughly $250 million in annual expenditure — about 40% of the Jewish community's spending in this area.

The ADL is the largest non-governmental trainer of US police and initiates exchanges between American police departments and Israeli military and police forces.

**Controversies:** In June 2024, Wikipedia editors voted to classify the ADL as "unreliable" on topics of antisemitism and the Israel-Gaza conflict. In June 2025, the National Education Association (NEA), the largest labor union in the United States, voted to cut ties with the ADL. The ADL has been criticized for brushing aside Elon Musk's apparent Nazi salute as "an awkward gesture" while labeling Gaza protesters "supporters of terror." Staff members have reportedly said that the organization's advocacy work has diverted it from its historical fight against antisemitism.

Jewish Voice for Peace, a prominent anti-Zionist Jewish organization, describes the ADL as an "anti-Palestinian, pro-Israel lobbying group pretending to be a civil rights organization."

### 5.2 The AJC and Pro-Israel Lobbying Organizations

The American Jewish Committee (AJC) promotes the IHRA definition and opposes both the JDA and the Nexus Document. It has gone so far as to designate the IHRA examples as "essentially the definition" — a position that elevates the Israel-related examples above the core definition itself.

Other organizations in this ecosystem include AIPAC (American Israel Public Affairs Committee), CAMERA (Committee for Accuracy in Middle East Reporting and Analysis), and the Simon Wiesenthal Center. The Wiesenthal Center made headlines when it listed Ben & Jerry's founders — an ice cream company that had opposed Israeli settlements — alongside Iran and Hamas in its "Global Anti-Semitism Top Ten."

### 5.3 Anti-Zionist and Palestinian Solidarity Groups

On the other side of the political spectrum, organizations like the Palestine Solidarity Campaign (UK), Jewish Voice for Peace (US), and IfNotNow (US) reject the ADL's framing and oppose the IHRA definition.

The Palestine Solidarity Campaign describes the IHRA definition as enabling "McCarthyite repression" and cautions that even the JDA still risks policing speech about Palestine.

IfNotNow made headlines by accusing AIPAC — a pro-Israel lobbying organization — of antisemitism for promoting conspiracy theories about George Soros.

### 5.4 Political Factions — The Left

The UK Labour Party under Jeremy Corbyn (2015-2020) became the site of one of the most extensively documented cases of antisemitism accusations being used as a factional weapon. The Forde Report, published in July 2022 after an investigation by barrister Martin Forde KC, found that "both factions treated antisemitism as a factional weapon." Anti-Corbyn elements "seized on antisemitism as a way to attack Jeremy Corbyn," while pro-Corbyn elements "saw it simply as an attack on the leader."

The report documented a "hierarchy of racism" in which antisemitism complaints received disproportionate resources while other forms of racism (anti-Black, Islamophobia) were ignored. It found that the Labour Party had no auditable database of antisemitism complaints and could not even count them accurately. Most complaints were generated by online factional trawls conducted by anti-Corbyn, non-Jewish individuals.

The scale of antisemitism in Labour was estimated at 1,803 investigated complaints over five years — a maximum of 0.37% of Labour members. The report concluded this could not be described as "widespread" and simply reflected prejudice levels found in wider society.

The Equality and Human Rights Commission (EHRC) investigation in October 2020 investigated 70 selected complaints, did NOT find "institutional antisemitism," and found unlawful harassment involving only two individuals — both of whom were challenging the allegations in court.

A 2018 YouGov poll found that 77% of Labour members believed the antisemitism issue was "deliberately exaggerated to damage Labour and Jeremy Corbyn or to stifle criticism of Israel."

After publishing the report, Forde gave an interview to Al Jazeera. The Labour Party responded by sending him a legal letter attempting to gag him. Forde described Labour's antisemitism training as "largely didactic, top-down and one-dimensional" and recommended that Jewish Voice for Labour be included in antisemitism education — a recommendation rejected by Keir Starmer.

In the United States, some progressive Democrats, including Representative Jan Schakowsky, have urged the State Department to adopt the JDA alongside IHRA.

### 5.5 Political Factions — The Right

The German far-right uses the term *"Antisemitismuskeule"* — literally, the "antisemitism club" or "cudgel" — to claim that Jews weaponize antisemitism accusations against them. The German National Democratic Party (NDP) explicitly cites Norman Finkelstein's *The Holocaust Industry* in support of this claim.

The Hungarian government under Viktor Orbán campaigns against George Soros using antisemitic coded language while simultaneously claiming to fight antisemitism.

Christian Zionists, such as Pastor John Hagee, support Israel for eschatological reasons (they believe the gathering of Jews in Israel is a prerequisite for the Second Coming of Christ) while promoting antisemitic conspiracy theories. Hagee has said that Hitler was sent by God to murder Jews who refused to emigrate to Israel.

White nationalists in Europe and the United States sometimes support Zionism as a means to expel Jews from their own countries — a phenomenon sometimes called "Zionist antisemitism."

### 5.6 Governments and International Bodies

The US State Department embraces the IHRA definition "in its entirety, including its examples." The United Nations passed Resolution 3379 in 1975 ("Zionism is racism"), revoked it in 1991. Canada has published an extensive handbook on the IHRA definition. Australia established a Royal Commission on Antisemitism (headed by Virginia Bell) which reaffirmed IHRA, though the ABC and SBS (public broadcasters) rejected its adoption.

## Part VI: Weaponization — When the Term Becomes a Weapon

### 6.1 The Livingstone Formulation

In 2005 and 2006, British sociologist David Hirsh coined the term "Livingstone Formulation," named after Ken Livingstone, the former Mayor of London. It describes a specific rhetorical maneuver: responding to an accusation of antisemitism with a counter-claim that the accuser is weaponizing antisemitism to suppress criticism of Israel.

Hirsh identified four key elements of the formulation:
1. Refuse to discuss the content of the accusation; shift focus to the accuser's hidden motive.
2. Counter-accuse the accuser of getting it wrong on purpose.
3. Collapse everything into the category of "legitimate criticism."
4. Allege that those raising concerns about antisemitism are part of a secret plan to silence criticism of Israel.

Hirsh traced the roots of this formulation back to the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion*, which accused Jews of "inventing or being the cause of antisemitism." Henry Ford complained in 1921 of the "degrading charge of 'anti-semitism' and kindred falsehoods." In 1952, the Soviet show trial of Rudolf Slánský included a forced "confession" that Zionists accused their critics of antisemitism in order to "shield Zionism" and "smear the left."

Hirsh cited historical precedents including Mahmoud Ahmadinejad, David Duke, Nick Griffin, Charles Lindbergh, Heinrich von Treitschke, and Wilhelm Marr himself.

In 2026, Australian Supreme Court Justice Fagan described IHRA promotion as having a "parasitic" purpose of suppressing protest against Israel. Hirsh cited this as a "striking judicial example" of the Livingstone Formulation.

### 6.2 The "Antisemitism Industrial Complex"

In 2024, academics John Harfouch (University of Alabama, Huntsville) and C. Heike Schotten published an article in the *Journal for the Critical Study of Zionism* titled "Sayegh's Critique of Zionism and the IHRA Definition: Notes Toward a Theory of the Antisemitism Industrial Complex."

The concept draws on Norman Finkelstein's earlier critique of the "Holocaust Industry" — the argument that Zionist organizations have created an exploitative mechanism that weaponizes Holocaust memorialization to extort money and political favors. The "antisemitism industrial complex" extends this logic: a network of powerful actors with financial and material incentives to continue producing the problem they ostensibly exist to alleviate.

The mechanism works, in this view, by displacing factual discussions of state violence with affective, abstract claims about antisemitism. The material impacts alleged include expedited weapons transfers to Israel, authoritarian domestic policy (such as "Project Esther" in the US), and the repression of protesters, universities, and students.

The concept quotes the Palestinian diplomat Fayez Sayegh, who wrote in 1960: "if anti-Jewishness did not exist, Zionists would have to create it."

### 6.3 The European Legal Support Center Findings

The European Legal Support Center (ELSC) examined 53 cases between 2017 and 2022 in which the IHRA definition was invoked in legal or institutional contexts. Their findings were striking:

- 11 cases involved accusations against Jews or Jewish groups.
- 42 cases involved accusations against groups or individuals of color, including 19 against Palestinian groups.
- The report concluded: "In the overwhelming majority of cases, allegations of antisemitism invoking the IHRA WDA are false."
- The IHRA WDA "has been adopted and implemented in a manner facilitating and validating the suppression of human rights advocacy for Palestinian rights and silencing criticism of Israeli government policies and practices."

### 6.4 The Forde Report: Documented Factional Weaponization

The Forde Report on the UK Labour Party, published in July 2022, remains one of the most thoroughly documented cases of antisemitism accusations being used as a factional weapon in a major political party.

The 138-page report, authored by Black barrister Martin Forde KC, contained 165 recommendations. Its central finding was that "both factions treated antisemitism as a factional weapon." The anti-Corbyn faction "seized on antisemitism as a way to attack Jeremy Corbyn," while the pro-Corbyn faction "saw it simply as an attack on the leader."

The report documented:
- A "hierarchy of racism" in which antisemitism complaints received disproportionate resources while other forms of racism were ignored.
- Senior staff displaying "deplorably factional and insensitive, and at times discriminatory, attitudes" toward Corbyn and his supporters, revealed in WhatsApp messages.
- The Labour Party having no auditable database of antisemitism complaints and being unable to count them accurately.
- Most complaints being generated by online factional trawls by anti-Corbyn, non-Jewish individuals.
- An estimated 1,803 investigated complaints over five years — a maximum of 0.37% of members.
- The EHRC finding no "institutional antisemitism" and unlawful harassment involving only two individuals.
- The BBC Panorama documentary on Labour antisemitism being "entirely misleading" in its presentation.

After publishing the report, Forde gave an interview to Al Jazeera. The Labour Party responded by sending him a legal letter attempting to gag him. Forde described Labour's antisemitism training as "largely didactic, top-down and one-dimensional" and recommended that Jewish Voice for Labour be included in antisemitism education — a recommendation rejected by Keir Starmer.

### 6.5 Norman Finkelstein and The Holocaust Industry

Norman Finkelstein, an American political scientist and the son of Holocaust survivors, published *The Holocaust Industry: Reflections on the Exploitation of Jewish Suffering* in 2000. The book remains one of the most controversial and widely cited critiques of how the memory of the Holocaust has been institutionalized.

Finkelstein's core argument is that a "Holocaust industry" has emerged — an alliance of Jewish organizations, lawyers, and politicians that exploits the memory of the Holocaust for political and financial gain. He argues that this industry has fabricated or exaggerated claims for reparations, particularly from Swiss banks and German corporations, and has used the moral authority of the Holocaust to silence criticism of Israel.

The book was condemned by many Jewish organizations and scholars. Holocaust historian Raul Hilberg, initially supportive, later distanced himself. Finkelstein was denied tenure at DePaul University in 2007 after a campaign by Alan Dershowitz, and was subsequently banned from entering Israel for ten years.

Despite the controversy, Finkelstein's argument has been cited by figures across the political spectrum — from the German far-right (who use it to claim Jews manipulate guilt) to anti-Zionist activists (who use it to argue that antisemitism accusations are deployed strategically). The concept of an "antisemitism industrial complex" developed by Harfouch and Schotten in 2024 is an explicit extension of Finkelstein's framework.

### 6.6 David Hirsh and the Engage Project

David Hirsh, the sociologist who coined the "Livingstone Formulation," has been one of the most prominent academic defenders of the IHRA definition and critics of what he sees as the left's failure to take antisemitism seriously.

Hirsh founded the website Engage in 2005 as a response to what he viewed as the growing acceptance of antisemitic discourse on the British left, particularly within the academic boycott of Israel movement. Engage positioned itself as a left-wing, anti-racist organization that opposed antisemitism from a progressive standpoint — arguing that antisemitism was not only a problem of the right but had infected the left through anti-Zionism.

Hirsh's work has been influential in shaping the argument that anti-Zionism and antisemitism are not always distinct, and that the left's framing of Israel as a uniquely evil state often borrows from classic antisemitic tropes. Critics argue that Hirsh's framework is too quick to label criticism of Israel as antisemitic and that Engage has functioned as a vehicle for attacking pro-Palestinian activists rather than genuinely combating racism.

The Engage project illustrates the intellectual architecture behind the weaponization debate: Hirsh accuses the left of weaponizing the "weaponization" accusation (the Livingstone Formulation), while his critics accuse him of weaponizing antisemitism to silence Palestinian solidarity.

## Part VII: The "Self-Hating Jew" and the "As-a-Jew" Phenomenon

### 7.1 The "Self-Hating Jew"

The accusation that a Jewish person is a "self-hating Jew" functions as a parallel weapon to the antisemitism accusation. Both are used to dismiss critics without engaging with the substance of their arguments.

Theodor Herzl, in his 1896 book *The Jewish State*, criticized enemies of Zionism as "disguised anti-Semites of Jewish origin." In 1898, Karl Straus turned the term back on Herzl himself, suggesting that Herzl was preoccupied with Jewish difference in a manner similar to traditional antisemites.

In 1930, Theodor Lessing published *Der Jüdische Selbsthass* ("Jewish Self-Hatred"), slinging the term at academics opposed to Zionism and describing his own journey from Jewish self-loathing to Zionism.

In 1941, German Jewish psychologist Kurt Lewin — who had immigrated to the United States in 1932 — published an essay titled "Self-Hatred among Jews" in *Contemporary Jewish Record*. Lewin defined Jewish self-hatred as a phenomenon in which Jews, regarded as a minority or "other" by host societies, came to resent and distance themselves from all things Jewish.

In 1942, historian Salo W. Baron coined the term "inverted Marranos" to describe Jews who "appear and act outwardly as Jews" but "deeply resent this fact." In 1948, Jean-Paul Sartre, in his book *Anti-Semite and Jew*, coined the term "inauthentic Jew" to describe a similar phenomenon.

In 1959, the writer Philip Roth was accused of being a "self-hating Jew" after *The New Yorker* published his story "Defender of the Faith." He was accused of causing "irreparable damage to the Jewish people," as much harm as "all the organized anti-Semitic organizations."

In 1949, writer Isaac Rosenfeld was accused after his essay "Adam and Eve on Delancey Street." Zionist Carl Alpert compared the effects of Rosenfeld's writing to "the best efforts of [Nazi propagandists] Streicher and Goebbels."

In 1957, scholar Sol Liptzin pointed to "the vogue of Jewish self-hatred" in American Jewish literature, singling out Ben Hecht, Budd Schulberg, and Michael Gold. The ADL picketed Arthur Laurents' play *Home of the Brave* (1949), which depicted a self-loathing Jewish soldier paralyzed by internalized antisemitism.

During the 1960s and 70s, "militant" Zionists used "Jewish self-hatred" to castigate critics of Jewish statehood. Hannah Arendt was accused of Jewish self-hatred after the publication of *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963).

During the Black Power era, Jewish writers argued that the integration of African Americans into Jewish neighborhoods threatened Jewish "apartness." "Self-respecting Jews" understood this; "self-hating Jews" did not.

Today, the widest usage of the term is in relation to debates over Israel. Right-wing Zionists assert that Zionism and support for Israel are core elements of Jewish identity. Jewish criticism of Israeli policy is therefore framed not as political disagreement but as a turning away from Jewish identity itself.

### 7.2 Academic Critique of "Self-Hating Jew"

The concept of "Jewish self-hatred" has been criticized for essentializing Jewish identity — assuming that a "superordinate Jewish identity should take precedence over other groupings of Jews."

Sander Gilman, in his 1986 book *Jewish Self-Hatred*, described the phenomenon as occurring in the space between "how Jews see the dominant society seeing them, and how they project their anxiety about this manner of being seen onto other Jews as a means of externalizing their own status anxiety."

Israel Shahak, a Polish-Israeli Holocaust survivor and chemistry professor, put it more bluntly: "That is a Nazi expression. The Nazis called Germans who defended Jewish rights self-hating Germans."

The academic consensus is that the accusation is too often deployed selectively: "Those who accuse others of being self-haters search for examples of when they have criticized Jews or Judaism but ignore examples of when those they criticize have shown they value being a Jew."

### 7.3 The "As-a-Jew" Phenomenon

A related and increasingly common rhetorical move is the "as-a-Jew" phenomenon: Jewish individuals who publicly criticize Israel or Zionism preface their statements with "As a Jew..." or "As a Jewish person..." This is intended to deflect accusations of antisemitism by establishing insider status.

The move is controversial. Supporters argue that it is necessary to demonstrate that criticism of Israel is not motivated by hatred of Jews, and that Jewish voices are often excluded from mainstream discourse precisely because they challenge the dominant narrative. Critics, including David Hirsh, argue that it is a form of identity politics that uses Jewishness as a shield for political positions that may still be antisemitic, and that it creates a false impression that the speaker represents Jewish opinion.

The phenomenon has become particularly visible on social media and in campus activism, where Jewish students involved in pro-Palestinian protests often wear shirts or carry signs identifying themselves as Jewish. The intention is to complicate the narrative that anti-Zionism is inherently antisemitic, but the effect is often to trigger counter-accusations of being a "self-hating Jew" or a "token."

## Part VIII: The Paradox of Zionist Antisemitism

### 8.1 Pro-Israel Antisemites

One of the most unsettling patterns in the history of antisemitism is the alliance between Zionists and antisemites. Theodor Herzl himself wrote in 1895: "Anti-Semites will become our most dependable friends, the anti-Semitic countries our allies."

This was not abstract. Historical examples include:

- **Wilhelm Marr**: The coiner of "antisemitism" supported Jewish migration to Palestine because it served German nationalist interests — it removed Jews from Germany.
- **Arthur Balfour**: The British Foreign Secretary who issued the Balfour Declaration supporting a Jewish homeland in Palestine also supported the Aliens Act of 1905, which restricted Jewish immigration to Britain. His support for Zionism was explicitly linked to his desire to reduce the "alien entity" of Jews in Britain.
- **The Polish government (1930s)**: Supported Zionism while adopting antisemitic domestic policies, encouraging Jewish emigration to decrease Poland's Jewish population.
- **Christian Zionists**: Support Israel for end-times prophecy while promoting antisemitic conspiracy theories about Jewish control of finance and media.
- **Far-right European nationalists**: Figures like Viktor Orbán and Anders Breivik support Israel as the "first line of defense against Muslim expansion" while holding antisemitic views about diaspora Jews.

### 8.2 Zionists Using Antisemitic Tropes

Early Zionist leaders sometimes employed language about diaspora Jews that echoed antisemitic caricatures. They contrasted the "new Jew" or "Hebrew" — muscular, self-reliant, rooted in the land — against the "Yid" — weak, parasitic, rootless. Ze'ev Jabotinsky described diaspora Jews in terms that echoed antisemitic stereotypes.

In modern politics, the Democratic Majority for Israel (DMFI) ran advertisements against Jewish politician Sara Jacobs emphasizing her "fortune and privileged life" — language that mirrored classic antisemitic tropes about Jewish wealth and power.

## Part IX: The Post-October 7 Campus Crisis

### 9.1 The Events

The Hamas attack on Israel on October 7, 2023, and Israel's subsequent military campaign in Gaza, triggered a crisis on university campuses across the United States and beyond that became, in many ways, a live experiment in how the term "antisemitism" functions in contemporary political discourse.

At Columbia University, the timeline unfolded as follows:

- **October 9, 2023**: Columbia chapters of Students for Justice in Palestine (SJP) and Jewish Voice for Peace (JVP) published an open letter calling October 7 "an unprecedented historic moment for Palestinians" and expressing "full solidarity with Palestinian resistance."
- **October 12**: A "Call to Action for Palestine" protest was organized by SJP, JVP, and other student groups.
- **October 18**: President Minouche Shafik published a statement addressing "terror attacks on Israel and the humanitarian crisis in Gaza."
- **October 25**: Doxing trucks funded by the conservative media organization Accuracy in Media circled the Columbia campus displaying the faces and names of pro-Palestine students under the heading "Columbia's Leading Antisemites."
- **November 1**: Columbia announced a Task Force on Antisemitism.
- **November 10**: SJP and JVP were suspended for violating campus event policies.
- **November 13**: An open letter signed by Zionist, anti-Zionist, and politically neutral students condemned the suspensions and called for working definitions that would "make space for legitimate criticism of Israel and Zionism."
- **November 15**: Faculty protested in solidarity with SJP and JVP, demanding that the university prevent "the equation of critiques of Israeli state policies and Zionist political ideology with antisemitism."
- **April 17, 2024**: President Shafik testified before Congress in a hearing titled "Columbia in Crisis: Columbia University's Response to Antisemitism."
- **April 18**: The NYPD arrested over 100 protesters at the Gaza Solidarity Encampment.
- **May 31**: Three deans were placed on leave after leaked text exchanges during an antisemitism panel were found to "disturbingly touch on antisemitic tropes."
- **August 14, 2024**: President Shafik resigned.

The New York Civil Liberties Union (NYCLU) demanded that Columbia reinstate SJP and JVP, citing free speech violations.

The crisis raised fundamental questions about where the line lies between protected political speech and harassment, between criticism of Israel and antisemitism, and between student activism and intimidation. It also revealed how the term "antisemitism" had become the primary battlefield on which these questions were fought.

## Part X: Legal, Free Speech, and Academic Freedom Implications

### 10.1 The Free Speech Problem

The adoption of the IHRA definition by institutions — universities, municipalities, corporations — creates what critics call a "de facto speech code." While the definition is explicitly non-legally binding, its institutional adoption shapes what can be said without professional or social consequence.

Constitutional expert Anne Twomey warned in 2026 of a "proliferation of laws" targeting antisemitism that lack clarity. In New South Wales, Australia, legislation criminalizing "imagery linked to Nazi ideology" was criticized as too vague: would wearing a black Helly Hansen jacket constitute a Nazi symbol?

In the United States, the First Amendment protects even Nazi speech. The 1977 Skokie case established that a Nazi march through a community with a large Holocaust survivor population was protected symbolic speech.

In 2025, Wisconsin introduced a bill that would legally adopt the IHRA definition. It was opposed by the ACLU and Jewish groups including Jewish Voice for Peace. Critics argued it would conflate legitimate criticism of Israel with antisemitism and potentially criminalize terms like "apartheid" or "genocide" — even though UN commissions and Israeli human rights organizations use such terms.

### 10.2 Academic Freedom

The adoption of IHRA by universities has been criticized as chilling criticism of Israel. At University College London (UCL), the Academic Board recommended replacing IHRA with an alternative definition. At the same time, 95 Jewish student activists signed a letter stating that they campaigned for IHRA adoption to protect Jewish students, "not the government of the State of Israel."

The tension is real and unresolved: how can universities protect Jewish students from genuine antisemitism while also protecting the academic freedom to criticize Israeli policy?

### 10.3 Legal Cases

In the UK case *Husain v SRA* [2025] EWHC 1170, Justice Chamberlain noted that the IHRA definition has been criticized as "incoherent, vague and vulnerable to political abuse" and confirmed that it has not been legally endorsed as a statutory definition.

The European Legal Support Center's analysis of 53 cases found that in the "overwhelming majority," allegations of antisemitism invoking IHRA were false, and the definition had been "adopted and implemented in a manner facilitating and validating the suppression of human rights advocacy for Palestinian rights."

## Part XI: What the Research Says — Psychology and Sociology

### 11.1 The Conspiracy Theory Structure

Multiple studies have established that antisemitism functions structurally as a conspiracy theory. The Executive Council of Australian Jewry describes it as "at its core a conspiracy theory" based on the irrational belief that "the Jews" — a tiny, historically marginalized group — conspire to cause harm. The two foundational myths are: Jews are innately evil, and Jews are immensely powerful. The target is often "mythological Jews rather than real Jews."

### 11.2 King's College London Study (2024)

A 2024 study by King's College London and the Alfred Landecker Foundation, published in *Nature Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, found that "antisemitism is predicted by anti-hierarchical aggression, totalitarianism, and belief in malevolent global conspiracies."

The study found that people with antisemitic attitudes toward Israel were more likely to have antisemitic attitudes toward British Jews. It provided strong evidence that antisemitism is predicted by a conspiratorial understanding of the world, openness to totalitarian rule, and a desire for revolution.

### 11.3 Rutgers University Study

A study by Kent Harber at Rutgers University, published in the *American Psychologist*, found a strong connection between conspiracy theories, antisemitic attitudes, and hostility toward Israel. Antisemitism channeled through conspiracy beliefs about Israel explained more than 55% of anti-Israel attitudes — what the researchers called a "very large effect for psychological research."

The study established a predictive relationship: antisemitic beliefs measured at the start of the study predicted conspiracy beliefs four weeks later, and conspiracy beliefs predicted anti-Israel attitudes four weeks after that. The researchers tested bogus conspiracy statements including "Israel created COVID-19 as a weapon against Palestine" and "Israel started the war in Gaza to summon the coming of a Jewish Messiah."

### 11.4 Malaysian Study

A study in Malaysia, where there is no established Jewish population, found that belief in Jewish conspiracy theories was associated with anti-Israeli attitudes, modern racism, right-wing authoritarianism, and social dominance orientation. The absence of actual Jews created what the researchers called an "imagined" or "perceived Jew" — a purely mythological target.

### 11.5 The Pattern

Across cultures and contexts, the research consistently shows that antisemitism is not primarily about Jews as real people. It is about a mythological construct — "the Jews" as a symbol of cosmic evil and hidden power — that serves psychological and political functions for the believer. This is why antisemitism persists even in places where no Jews live, and why it reappears in wildly different political contexts, from medieval Christianity to Soviet communism to contemporary QAnon.

## Part XII: Global Contexts

### 12.1 Germany's Memory Culture

Germany's approach to its Nazi past — known as *Vergangenheitsbewältigung*, or "coming to terms with the past" — is often cited as a model of national reckoning. Unlike many nations, Germany confronted its atrocities rather than denying or suppressing them.

However, critics argue that Germany's memory culture has created a paradox. The Arab Center in Washington, DC, argued in 2024 that Germany's approach to Palestinian identity aligns with "the most extreme and right-wing Zionist views." Those who oppose this stance, including many Jewish activists, are often labeled antisemitic.

Berlin-based Jewish activist Deborah Feldman has stated: "Only Jews who adhere to the official German creed and the most radical version of Zionism are considered true Jews and hence worthy of protection."

The targeting of Palestinian identity is linked to Germany's grappling with historical guilt. The persistence of Palestinian identity and resistance challenges the prevailing narrative of Germany's memory culture. In a striking anomaly, far-right parties with neo-Nazi ties can protest under police protection in Germany, while Jews who criticize Israeli policies are routinely arrested at peace demonstrations and charged with antisemitism.

### 12.2 The Arab and Muslim World

For decades, the prevailing assumption in Western scholarship was that antisemitism in the Arabic-speaking world emerged solely due to the rise of Nazi Germany. New scholarship challenges this, arguing that while Western influences played a significant role, antisemitism has deep roots in the social and cultural systems of Arabic-speaking communities, intensified by the creation of Israel, Nazi propaganda, and Soviet-era anti-Zionist narratives.

The term "Arab antisemitism" has increasingly been merged with or replaced by "Islamic antisemitism" or "Muslim antisemitism" with the rise of Islamist movements from the 1970s onward. The Muslim Brotherhood under Anwar Sadat in Egypt is identified as a key vector for a new form of antisemitism that arose after the 1967 war.

### 12.3 China

In China, antisemitism manifests primarily through conspiracy theories absorbed via globalized media rather than rooted historical hostility. Chinese state media and online forums have circulated claims about Jewish control of global finance, often blending them with nationalist narratives about Western imperialism. The absence of a significant Jewish population in China means that these beliefs function as abstract political symbols rather than personal prejudice. However, the Chinese government's complex relationship with Israel — balancing economic cooperation with support for Palestinian statehood — creates a contradictory discourse in which antisemitic tropes coexist with diplomatic engagement.

### 12.4 India

In India, antisemitism has intersected with Hindu nationalist discourse in complex ways. While India has historically been one of the few countries without a history of pogroms against Jews (the Bene Israel and Cochin Jewish communities lived relatively peacefully for centuries), contemporary Hindu nationalist movements have occasionally borrowed antisemitic tropes about global financial elites, often conflating them with anti-Muslim conspiracy theories. At the same time, some Hindu nationalists express strong support for Israel as a fellow "civilizational state" resisting Islamic influence. The result is a contradictory landscape in which antisemitic and philosemitic impulses coexist within the same political movements.

### 12.5 Latin America

In Latin America, antisemitism has intersected with Catholic anti-Judaism and, in some countries, with fascist movements. Argentina, which has the largest Jewish population in Latin America, experienced significant antisemitism under the military junta (1976-1983), during which Jewish dissidents were disproportionately targeted. Neo-Nazi groups have persisted in Brazil, Chile, and Argentina. More recently, left-wing movements in Latin America have sometimes adopted anti-Zionist rhetoric that borrows from Soviet-era propaganda, creating tensions with Jewish communities.

### 12.6 Africa

In Africa, antisemitism has been shaped by colonial legacies, Christian missionary activity, and more recently by geopolitical alignments. Some African nations with Muslim-majority populations have adopted anti-Zionist rhetoric that includes antisemitic tropes. South Africa's apartheid-era government had complex relations with Israel (including military cooperation) while also harboring antisemitic elements. Post-apartheid, the ruling ANC has been strongly critical of Israel, sometimes using rhetoric that Jewish groups have identified as antisemitic. The lack of comprehensive scholarship on antisemitism in sub-Saharan Africa represents a significant gap in the global understanding of the phenomenon.

## Part XIII: Holocaust Education and the Corporate World

### 13.1 Holocaust Education Globally

In 2020, UNESCO and the OSCE's Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights (ODIHR) launched "Addressing Anti-Semitism in Schools: Training Curricula" — four volumes for primary, secondary, vocational teachers, and school directors. The materials were developed with the University College London Centre for Holocaust Education.

In the United States, only 29 states mandate Holocaust education as of 2024. The ADL's "Echoes and Reflections" program, a partnership with the USC Shoah Foundation and Yad Vashem, has trained thousands of educators and reached approximately 125,000 educators and 12 million students since 2005.

An ADL survey in 2024 found that 84% of Americans support antisemitism education in K-12 schools, and 89% support Holocaust education. Yet only 21% of K-12 parents said their child's school offered antisemitism education, and only 30% said Holocaust education was offered. ADL data showed a 135% rise in antisemitic incidents in K-12 schools over the previous year.

In Canada, Ontario became the first province to mandate Holocaust learning in elementary schools (Grade 6) in 2022; previously, it had only been taught in Grade 10. A 2021 study by Western University and Liberation75 found that 42% of students surveyed had witnessed an antisemitic event, and one in three students thought the Holocaust was fabricated, exaggerated, or were unsure if it happened.

Controversies have emerged around curriculum content. In Pennsylvania, Senate Bill 127 (2025) would adopt the IHRA definition in Holocaust education requirements. It was opposed by the ACLU of Pennsylvania on the grounds that IHRA "encompasses protected speech" and conflates criticism of Israel with antisemitism. The AJC recommends including units on the Israel-Palestinian conflict presenting "multiple narratives and nuanced perspectives." The pro-Israel organization StandWithUs reviews curricula and professional development materials to assess whether there is "anti-Israel or antisemitic bias" and attempts to get curricula removed or revised.

### 13.2 Corporate and DEI Contexts

Antisemitism has increasingly entered the corporate world through Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) frameworks. Companies like Emtrain have developed workplace discrimination training specifically targeting antisemitism. The Brandeis Center runs a corporate initiative to train employees on antisemitism, Jewish identity, and rights in the workplace, and to counsel senior executives and DEI/HR professionals.

Project Shema trains DEI and HR professionals on how anti-Jewish ideas show up in discourse around Israel and Palestine. Jewish Employee Resource Groups (ERGs) have become more prominent, with organizations like the UJA-Federation of New York providing resources.

A paradox has emerged: Jewish employees are often excluded from DEI frameworks because Jews are sometimes classified as "white" in DEI contexts, erasing their minority status and vulnerability to antisemitism. The Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism explicitly identifies "treating Jews as though they were 'white' when they are targeted by white supremacists" as a form of antisemitism.

## Part XIV: The "Industry" — Funding, Incentives, and Economics

### 14.1 The Financial Ecosystem

Understanding who funds the organizations that define, monitor, and combat antisemitism is essential to understanding how the term is deployed.

The ADL operates on an annual budget of approximately $134 million (2024). The AJC and Robert Kraft's Foundation to Combat Antisemitism add roughly another $116 million. Combined, these three organizations represent approximately $250 million in annual expenditure — about 40% of the Jewish community's spending in this area. The median budget of organizations reviewed in this space is approximately $2 million, with a median program expenditure of $1.2 million.

On the other side, organizations critical of Israel and IHRA receive funding from the Rockefeller Brothers Fund, Open Society Foundations (founded by George Soros), the Tides Foundation, the Kataly Foundation, and the Sparkplug Foundation. These fund Jewish Voice for Peace, Palestine Legal, National Students for Justice in Palestine, the US Campaign for Palestinian Rights, and others.

### 14.2 The Incentive Structure

Every actor in this ecosystem has incentives that shape their behavior:

**For pro-Israel groups:** Documenting antisemitism validates the need for Israel as a safe haven, justifies military and financial aid to Israel, and delegitimizes critics. The more antisemitism there appears to be, the stronger the case for Zionism.

**For anti-Zionist groups:** Documenting exaggerated or false antisemitism claims validates the narrative that Jewish organizations wield disproportionate power and manipulate public discourse. This delegitimizes pro-Israel advocacy.

**For politicians:** Taking a strong stance against antisemitism signals moral virtue and can be used to attack opponents on both the left and the right.

**For media organizations:** Antisemitism stories generate engagement, clicks, and advertising revenue.

**For the organizations themselves:** Their funding, staffing, and influence depend on the continued perception of antisemitism as a serious and growing threat. This is not to say the threat is fabricated — the data on rising antisemitic incidents is real — but it is to say that the institutional response to the threat has created its own economy with its own incentives.

Norman Finkelstein's critique of the "Holocaust Industry" — that Zionist organizations have created an exploitative mechanism that weaponizes Jewish suffering for political and financial gain — remains one of the most controversial but widely cited analyses of this incentive structure.

## Part XV: Modern Movements and Discourses

### 15.1 QAnon, the Great Replacement, and Antisemitic Conspiracy Revival

The QAnon movement, which emerged in 2017, represents a modern revival of classic antisemitic conspiracy theories repackaged for the digital age. QAnon's core narrative — that a secret cabal of global elites runs a child sex trafficking ring and is plotting against Donald Trump — draws directly from the Protocols of the Elders of Zion and the blood libel.

The "adrenochrome" conspiracy, a central QAnon belief that elites harvest a chemical from children's blood to maintain their youth, is a direct descendant of the blood libel. The coded language about "globalists," "Soros-funded" operations, and "the elite" functions as a dog whistle for "Jews" that allows the conspiracy to spread in mainstream spaces without explicit antisemitic language.

The Great Replacement theory — the claim that white populations are being deliberately replaced by non-white immigrants through orchestrated migration — has similarly deep antisemitic roots. The theory, promoted by far-right figures across Europe and the United States, often identifies Jewish philanthropists (particularly George Soros) and organizations as the architects of this replacement. The 2018 Pittsburgh synagogue shooter explicitly cited the Great Replacement theory and accused HIAS, a Jewish refugee resettlement organization, of bringing "invaders in that kill our people."

These movements illustrate how antisemitism functions as a "ur-conspiracy theory" — a foundational explanatory framework that can be adapted to any political context. Whether the enemy is communists, immigrants, or "the elite," the structure remains the same: a small, hidden group with disproportionate power is orchestrating events from behind the scenes.

### 15.2 The Alt-Right and Online Antisemitism

The alt-right movement that emerged in the mid-2010s brought antisemitism into mainstream online discourse through irony, memes, and coded language. On platforms like 4chan's /pol/ board, 8chan, and later Telegram, antisemitic tropes were repackaged as "edgy" humor.

Key elements of alt-right antisemitic discourse include:
- **Triple parentheses** ((( ))): Used to identify Jewish individuals or organizations, allowing antisemites to target people without explicit slurs.
- **Pepe the Frog**: A cartoon character appropriated by the alt-right and used in antisemitic memes.
- **"The Jewish Question"**: A phrase borrowed from Nazi discourse, used to frame Jewish existence as a problem requiring a "solution."
- **"We wuz kangz"**: A mocking phrase used to ridicule Black history while simultaneously promoting white nationalist and antisemitic narratives.
- **Accelerationism**: The belief that societal collapse should be hastened, often with the goal of creating a white ethnostate.

The 2017 "Unite the Right" rally in Charlottesville, Virginia, where marchers chanted "Jews will not replace us," marked the movement's emergence from online spaces into physical violence. The movement's use of irony — claiming that Nazi salutes and Holocaust jokes are "just memes" — has made it difficult for platforms and institutions to respond effectively.

### 15.3 Postcolonial Theory and Antisemitism

Postcolonial studies, particularly the work of Edward Said in *Orientalism* (1978), has provided a framework for understanding how Western powers construct images of "the Other." However, the relationship between postcolonial theory and antisemitism is complex and contested.

Some postcolonial scholars have argued that Zionism is a form of European colonialism, and that the State of Israel is a settler-colonial project. This framing has been adopted by many pro-Palestinian activists and academics. Critics, including David Hirsh and others, argue that this framing erases the indigenous Middle Eastern history of Jews and borrows from antisemitic tropes by portraying Jews as foreign interlopers in their own ancestral homeland.

The "neo-lachrymose conception of Jewish-Arab history" — a term coined by historian Mark Cohen — describes the revisionist narrative that emphasizes the persecution of Jews under Islamic rule while downplaying centuries of relatively peaceful coexistence. This narrative, Cohen argues, was developed in part to rebut Arab claims that Zionism disrupted an idyllic Jewish-Muslim relationship.

The tension between postcolonial frameworks and Jewish history remains unresolved. Some Jewish scholars have embraced postcolonial theory to analyze the Mizrahi Jewish experience (Jews from Arab and Muslim countries), while others reject it as fundamentally hostile to Jewish self-determination.

### 15.4 Antisemitism in Film, Literature, and Popular Culture

Antisemitic tropes have persisted in Western popular culture for over a century, often in coded or unconscious forms.

**Early cinema:** D.W. Griffith's *Intolerance* (1916) included sympathetic depictions of Jews, but the broader trend in early Hollywood was toward stereotypical portrayals. *The Jazz Singer* (1927), while celebrated as a milestone for Jewish representation, centered on a Jewish performer who achieves success by assimilating into mainstream (Christian) culture — a narrative that implicitly devalued Jewish identity.

**Mid-century:** Films like *Gentleman's Agreement* (1947) and *Crossfire* (1947) addressed antisemitism directly, but often from a perspective that emphasized Jewish assimilation as the solution. The "Jewish mother" stereotype, the "neurotic Jewish intellectual," and the "greedy Jewish businessman" became stock characters in American film and television.

**Contemporary media:** More recent films and television shows have grappled with antisemitism in complex ways. *The Plot Against America* (2020), based on Philip Roth's novel, imagined an alternate history in which Charles Lindbergh — an aviator with documented antisemitic views — became president. Shows like *Curb Your Enthusiasm* and *The Marvelous Mrs. Maisel* have explored Jewish identity with varying degrees of self-awareness.

However, antisemitic tropes persist in subtler forms. The portrayal of Jewish characters as wealthy, neurotic, or controlling — even in ostensibly sympathetic roles — reinforces stereotypes. The underrepresentation of Mizrahi, Ethiopian, and other non-Ashkenazi Jews in Western media creates a monolithic image of "the Jew" that erases diversity within the Jewish world.

The streaming era has also seen the rise of explicitly antisemitic content on platforms with minimal moderation, as well as the use of antisemitic tropes in "anti-establishment" media that targets "global elites" without naming Jews explicitly.

## Part XVI: Gaps, Anomalies, and Patterns

### 16.1 Gaps in the Record

- **No comprehensive philosophical analysis of the IHRA definition existed until 2021.** For a definition adopted by over 35 countries and 1,100 entities, this is a striking gap.
- **No auditable database of antisemitism complaints.** The Forde Report found that the UK Labour Party — one of the world's major political parties — could not even count its antisemitism complaints accurately.
- **The "dark figure" of underreporting.** Both online and offline antisemitic incidents are substantially underreported. We do not know the true scale.
- **No parent Semitic language was ever found.** The linguistic category on which the term is built rests on a philological gap.
- **No alternative term exists for prejudice by Arabs against Jews.** Encyclopedia Britannica notes this explicitly.
- **Psychological research is mostly correlational, not causal.** We know that antisemitism is associated with conspiracy theories and authoritarianism, but the causal pathways are not fully established.
- **No global legal standard exists.** The IHRA definition is explicitly non-legally binding, and different countries adopt different frameworks.
- **Corporate and HR data is lacking.** Little research exists on how antisemitism manifests in workplace DEI contexts.
- **Non-Western perspectives are underrepresented.** Most scholarship centers on European and North American experience.
- **Pop culture and media representation remain understudied.** While antisemitic tropes in film and literature have been analyzed, the term's deployment in contemporary media discourse has not been systematically mapped.

### 16.2 Anomalies — Things That Do Not Fit the Standard Narrative

- **Wilhelm Marr recanted.** The man who coined "antisemitism" apologized for his "mistaken notions" in 1891. The father of the ideology rejected it.
- **Herzl sought antisemites as allies.** The founder of political Zionism wrote that "anti-Semites will become our most dependable friends."
- **The Soviet Union used Nazi conspiracy theories.** A far-left regime repackaged far-right racist ideas as "anti-Zionist" critique.
- **Everyone accuses everyone else of weaponizing the term.** The pattern is symmetrical: pro-Israel groups accuse critics of antisemitism; critics accuse pro-Israel groups of weaponizing the accusation; the far-right accuses Jews of weaponizing it; Jews accuse the far-right of using the accusation to cover their own hatred.
- **Britannica calls it a misnomer.** The most authoritative English-language encyclopedia acknowledges the term is technically wrong, yet it is universally used.
- **The ADL spied on civil rights groups.** An organization founded to fight hate conspired with police to spy on the NAACP, the ACLU, Arab-American groups, and anti-apartheid activists.
- **The Forde gag attempt.** A major political party tried to legally silence its own appointed investigator after he published findings it did not like.
- **Pro-Israel activists value the examples more than the definition.** The 38-word IHRA core definition is considered "neutered" by the Simon Wiesenthal Center; the 11 examples are treated as "essentially the definition."
- **The Wiesenthal Center listed ice cream company founders alongside Iran and Hamas.** Ben & Jerry's founders were named in the "Global Anti-Semitism Top Ten" for opposing Israeli settlements.
- **Leo Frank was the only Jew ever lynched in America.** He was also the first white man in the Deep South tried on a capital charge based solely on the testimony of a Black man.
- **The "self-hating Jew" accusation mirrors Nazi rhetoric.** The Nazis called Germans who defended Jewish rights "self-hating Germans." The same rhetorical structure is now used against Jewish critics of Israel.
- **Germany's memory culture is used to silence Jewish critics of Israel.** The nation that perpetrated the Holocaust arrests Jews at peace demonstrations while protecting neo-Nazi protests.
- **The ADL's origin myth may be false.** Recent scholarship by Emmaia Gelman questions whether the ADL was founded in response to the Leo Frank case, suggesting instead it was formed by midwestern German Jews worried about Eastern European Jewish immigrants changing perceptions of Jewishness.
- **The same term is used by all sides.** Everyone — Zionists, anti-Zionists, the far-right, the far-left, governments, and private citizens — uses "antisemitism" to describe both genuine hatred and political disagreement.

### 16.3 Patterns — What the Evidence Shows

- **The term evolves with political needs.** It began as religious hatred (*Judenhass*), became racial "science" (Marr), became political ideology (Nazi), became anti-Zionist propaganda (Soviet), and is now a campus and online discourse weapon.
- **Every accusation is met with a counter-accusation.** This pattern dates back to the 19th century and shows no sign of breaking.
- **The left and right both use it.** Antisemitism accusations are not a partisan phenomenon. They are used by all political factions against their enemies.
- **Israel/Palestine dominates modern usage.** Most contemporary debates about antisemitism center on Israel, not on traditional Jew-hatred.
- **Inflation dilutes the term.** As the term expands to cover anti-Zionism, BDS, and certain criticisms of Israel, the risk increases that it will lose its power to describe genuine hatred of Jews.
- **Institutional capture has occurred.** The battle over which definition (IHRA vs. JDA vs. Nexus) institutions adopt has become a proxy war for Israel/Palestine politics.
- **Antisemitism consistently functions as a conspiracy theory.** The structure is always the same: Jews are simultaneously all-powerful and vulnerable, controlling everything yet needing to be exposed.
- **Psychological predictors are consistent.** Across cultures, antisemitism is linked to general conspiracist ideation, authoritarianism, social dominance orientation, and desire for revolution.
- **The "self-hatred" accusation mirrors the antisemitism accusation.** Both are used to dismiss critics without engaging their arguments.
- **Memory culture becomes a weapon.** Germany's *Vergangenheitsbewältigung* is used to silence Palestinian solidarity and Jewish critics of Israel.
- **The term is profitable.** Organizations on all sides of the debate have built substantial budgets around the monitoring, defining, and combating of antisemitism.
- **The "as-a-Jew" phenomenon and the "self-hating Jew" accusation form a closed loop.** One is used to claim legitimacy; the other is used to deny it. Neither addresses the substance of the argument.

## Conclusion: What We Know and What We Do Not

After multiple deep-dive research passes across academic journals, government reports, court records, encyclopedias, organizational publications, news archives, psychological studies, and cultural analyses, what can we say with confidence?

**We know:**
- The term "antisemitism" was coined in 1879 by Wilhelm Marr, a German nationalist who wanted to give Jew-hatred a pseudo-scientific, racial facade. It was never intended to apply to all Semitic peoples. It is, by the admission of Encyclopedia Britannica, a misnomer.
- The term has been used by Nazis, Soviets, Zionists, anti-Zionists, the far-right, the far-left, governments, and private citizens. It has been used to describe genuine hatred of Jews and to silence legitimate political criticism.
- The IHRA definition, the most widely adopted framework, is controversial, legally non-binding, and has been criticized as "incoherent, vague and vulnerable to political abuse" by a UK High Court judge.
- There is documented evidence of the term being used as a factional weapon in major political parties, most thoroughly documented in the UK Labour Party's Forde Report.
- There is a multi-hundred-million-dollar ecosystem of organizations whose funding and influence depend on the continued perception of antisemitism as a serious threat.
- Psychological research consistently links antisemitism to conspiracy theories, authoritarianism, and social dominance orientation.
- The term's deployment in the context of Israel and Palestine has become the dominant battlefield in contemporary discourse, often obscuring the distinction between genuine Jew-hatred and political disagreement.
- Modern movements from QAnon to the alt-right to the Great Replacement theory have revived and repackaged classic antisemitic tropes for the digital age.
- Postcolonial theory, film and literature, and corporate DEI frameworks have all become sites of contestation over the meaning and application of the term.

**We do not know:**
- The true scale of antisemitism, due to massive underreporting.
- Whether the IHRA definition does more good than harm in institutional contexts.
- The full extent of funding flows in the "antisemitism industry" on all sides.
- How antisemitism manifests in non-Western contexts, where scholarship is limited.
- Whether the inflation of the term will eventually render it meaningless.
- How to protect Jewish students and communities from genuine hatred while preserving the freedom to criticize Israeli policy.
- Whether the "as-a-Jew" phenomenon and the "self-hating Jew" accusation will continue to function as parallel silencing tools.

What is clear is that the word "antisemitism" is not a neutral diagnostic tool. It is a term with a specific political origin, a contested meaning, a documented history of misuse, and a present-day function as both a shield against hatred and a sword against dissent. Understanding it requires understanding all of these dimensions — not adopting the theories of others, but gathering the clues and evidence and following them where they lead.
